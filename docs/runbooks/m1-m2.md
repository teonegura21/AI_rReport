# What only your pod can measure: the M1 and M2 runbook

rReport's intelligence core is built and proven in CI with fakes, a deterministic oracle model and fault injection. What real models do on a real GPU can only be measured on your pod. This runbook lists those measurements, R1 to R6, in the order in which the implementation phases make them possible. For each one it says what to run, what to look for, and what to send back.

**Before any of them:** the [M0 runbook](../../deploy/runpod/README.md) done once: the model checksums checked and pinned (its step 1), the image built, the network volume, the pod and the hub configured.

**Sending results back.** Commit the files a step names to `docs/runbooks/results/<step>/` on the branch, or attach them to a message. JSON files from `rreport bench` hold no secrets: model ids, timings, token counts, the GPU's name.

| Step | After | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| [R1](#r1-the-commanders-slots-and-the-prompt-cache) | P5 | The commander's parallel slots, its shared pool, and the prompt cache |
| [R2](#r2-the-evaluation-videos-and-their-gold-annotations) | P16 | The evaluation videos and their gold annotations |
| R3 | P22 | M1's acceptance: three videos ingested at the `max` tier, and read |
| R4 | P25 | The tiers measured against each other, with replicates and an A/A run |
| R5 | P27 | The evaluation's judges, calibrated against your labels |
| R6 | P27 | M2's acceptance: ablations, then the gate on the test split |

R3 to R6 are written when their phase lands, because each depends on commands that do not exist yet.

## R1. The commander's slots and the prompt cache

**Why.** On the 48 and 80 GB profiles, the commander now runs **4 slots that share one pool** of attention cache, where it had 1 slot ([runtime.md](../design/runtime.md#context-per-slot-and-the-shared-pool) explains the pool). The pipeline depends on that in three ways, and each is measured here:

1. **Requests at once.** The pipeline sends the commander several missions at once, and asks it for several answers per request (k drafts, as `n`). Decoding a 27B model is limited by memory bandwidth: every token reads all the weights once, whatever the number of sequences sharing the step. So four streams should give about three times the tokens per second of one. How much they really give sets what the `max` tier's drafts cost in time.
2. **The prompt cache.** The pipeline's prompts put everything shared in the system message (the role, the rules, the video's brief) and vary only the last user message, so that the engine can serve the shared part from its cache. Qwen3.8 is a hybrid model, partly recurrent, and can reuse a prefix only from checkpoints. Whether it does, one request after another and for requests at once, is a question only the pod can answer.
3. **Long prompts.** Outlining reads a whole transcript, up to about 20,000 tokens for a 90-minute podcast. With 4 slots splitting 32,768 tokens evenly, nothing over 8,192 would fit; in a shared pool, one sequence may use all of it.

**Steps, on a 48 GB pod** (L40S or RTX A6000):

1. **Rebuild the image** from this branch (GitHub: **Actions → runtime-image → Run workflow**, then pick the branch). The hub ↔ runtime protocol is now v2: this hub refuses a runtime built before the change, and `rreport doctor` says to update both.
2. **Start the pod and check the commander.**
   ```sh
   rreport pod start
   rreport pod status
   ```
   The commander's line should read:
   ```
   qwen3.8-27b-q4 (commander): ready, 4 slot(s), up to 32768 tokens each from a shared pool
   ```
   A smaller figure means llama.cpp capped the context: the runtime's log has a line `engine 'qwen3.8-27b-q4': llama-server gives each slot … tokens` that says by how much.
3. **The speeds, k answers per request, and the cache:**
   ```sh
   rreport bench --commander-parallel 1,2,4 --n 3 --cache-check --out r1-48gb.json
   ```
   It takes a few minutes and prints Markdown tables.
4. **A long prompt:**
   ```sh
   rreport bench --prompt-tokens 20000 --output-tokens 256 --rounds 2 \
     --commander-parallel 1,2 --parallel 1 --out r1-48gb-long.json
   ```
   Three results are expected here, and two of them are failures, which the tables keep as results:
   - the commander's one-at-a-time row runs: 20,000 tokens fit its pool of 32,768;
   - its "2 at once" level should fail with "Context size has been exceeded": two such requests do not fit the pool together. This is the limit the pipeline budgets for: llama-server fails every request in flight when it is crossed, and carries on. If the level runs instead, say so: the pool would then hold more than its size suggests;
   - the infantry's level fails, refused up front: 20,000 tokens exceed its 4,096 per slot.

**On an 80 GB pod** (A100 or H100), run the same two benchmarks, naming the files `r1-80gb.json` and `r1-80gb-long.json`. There the commander's pool holds 65,536 tokens, so "2 at once" at 20,000 tokens fits.

**What to look for:**

| Table | Healthy | If not |
|---|---|---|
| Engines | The commander: 4 slots, `per slot` equal to `context`, pool `shared` | Rebuild the image from this branch; check `pod status` |
| Commander, several requests at once | Total tokens per second at 4 at once is 2.5 or more times the figure at 1 | Between 1.5× and 2.5×: fine, and noted. Below 1.5×: the GPU is compute-bound at this model size; MTP speculative decoding (P29) becomes the experiment to run |
| Commander, 3 answers per request | The generated speed is well above the one-answer speed | Send the JSON: `n` on a shared pool should read the prompt once |
| Prompt cache | `qwen3.8-27b-q4: reused, also by 4 requests at once` | "reused one request at a time, NOT by 4 at once": each slot reads the shared part again, so the pipeline will send a stage's first mission alone and the rest after it. "NOT reused": send the runtime's log from the bench's time |
| Peak VRAM | At least 2 GB below the card's total | Send the JSON: the profile's context or slots will shrink |

**Send back** (to `docs/runbooks/results/r1/`):
- the four JSON files (`r1-48gb.json`, `r1-48gb-long.json`, `r1-80gb.json`, `r1-80gb-long.json`);
- from the runtime's log (RunPod console → the pod → Logs), the lines that start with `engine '`, if any.

**What happens next.** The numbers fix the commander's slots and context in `docker/runtime/catalog.toml` and the tiers' time estimates. The cache verdict fixes how the army orders a stage's missions (P8).

## R2. The evaluation videos and their gold annotations

**Why.** M2 measures every article against what its video says, written down once by a person ([eval.md](../design/eval.md)). This step makes that data: the set of videos (your approval), their captions frozen (snapshots), and their gold annotations, which models draft and you correct. It takes about an hour of your time per hour of video, most of it reading.

**Steps:**

1. **Approve the candidates.** [`eval/README.md`](../../eval/README.md#approving-the-candidates) says how to check each one on YouTube and choose. Then write `eval/sets/m2.toml`, one `[[video]]` per approved video ([eval.md](../design/eval.md#the-set) has the format), or say which you approve and it will be written for you. Mark about a third `split = "test"`, across the types.
2. **Fetch the snapshots**, on any machine with yt-dlp (the laptop is fine; no pod):
   ```sh
   rreport eval fetch
   ```
   Each video prints where it was kept and the line that pins it:
   ```
   fed-talk-1: kept eval/data/fed-talk-1/fed-talk-1.source.json, snapshot 1acc46b4…
     pin it in the set, under the video: snapshot = "1acc46b4…"
   ```
   Add each `snapshot = "…"` line to its video in `eval/sets/m2.toml`. A video refused here says why: captions of the other kind than the set says (correct the set, or choose another video), or a CC BY video whose page shows no Creative Commons line.
3. **Draft the gold annotations**, with the pod configured (`rreport doctor` all ok):
   ```sh
   rreport eval annotate
   ```
   The pod starts if it is stopped. For each video, the commander drafts every stretch three times, thinking, and the infantry adds what they missed; expect several minutes per video-hour. It prints, per video, how many facts were drafted and the command for its review sheet. An interrupted run is taken up by running the same command again: nothing already answered is asked again.
4. **Correct each one into gold.** For each video:
   ```sh
   rreport eval sheet fed-talk-1 --out fed-talk-1.md
   ```
   Open the sheet beside `eval/gold/fed-talk-1.toml`, and the video beside both: each fact's time is a link to its moment. In the TOML file:
   - check each claim against its quote, and the quote against the video. Doubt first the facts marked `?` (only one of the three drafts found them) and those with `found = 0` (the other model's additions);
   - set `importance`: `core` when an article without the fact would miss the point, `detail` otherwise;
   - add what the drafts missed (a new `[[fact]]` with an id not used yet, `F40`), merge or split facts, delete what the video does not say. Keep the ids of the facts you keep;
   - keep the must-quotes that an article must quote word for word, and the terms the video uses;
   - when it is right, set `status = "gold"` and `reviewer` to your name.

   Run the sheet again after correcting: its last section, "To correct", lists every quote not said where its fact says, and anything else the sheet can check. Stop when it says "Nothing the sheet can check".

**What to look for:**

| Sign | Healthy | If not |
|---|---|---|
| `eval fetch` | every video kept | A video without captions in the language spoken, or of the other kind: choose another, or correct the set |
| `eval annotate` findings | none, or a few windows "no answer passed its checks" | Many: send the job's id (`rreport jobs`) and the command's output; the prompts' failure modes are in `prompts/manifest.toml` |
| The drafts | most facts `found = 3`; few `?` | Many `?` facts: the drafts disagree, and the window size or the thinking budget may be wrong for the pod; send `rreport jobs show <job>` |
| Your corrections | under about a third of the facts changed | More: the prompts miss what matters; your corrected files are exactly what improves them |

**Send back:** commit `eval/sets/m2.toml`, `eval/data/` and `eval/gold/` on the branch. They hold captions, descriptions and your annotations of public-domain and CC BY videos only, and no secret.

**What happens next.** The gold files are what P23–P25 score articles against, and their `found` counts and your corrections measure the drafting itself: how much a person had to fix is the annotation prompts' own eval.
